# Worked Example — Washington, DC (city and landmarks)

An illustrative example of a non-outdoor trip. The group details are representative, not a real family. Facts about specific attractions are examples of the kind of thing to research, not verified current information.

## Group
Two adults and three children (roughly 6–12); first visit to DC; 4 nights; flying in; interests: famous landmarks, museums, history, kid-focused activities. Several answers were “not sure”: one base or two, rental car or transit, budget.

## How the open decisions were resolved
- **Getting around (was “not sure”):** at the logistics step the planner compared a rental car (parking costs and traffic) against Metro + walking + occasional rideshare. Recommended: no car, stay near a Metro line. The user agreed.
- **Bases (was “not sure”):** compared one hotel near the National Mall, one in Arlington near Metro (cheaper, larger rooms) and splitting the stay. Recommended: one Arlington base. The user agreed; it was recorded as AGREED, not LOCKED.
- **Budget (was “help me estimate”):** the planner showed per-person-per-day ranges for lodging, food and activities alongside the options; the user then set a per-day budget for the whole group, excluding flights.

## Key calibration lesson
“The kids love museums” turned out to mean about 2 hours before attention dropped, not a full day. Calibrated plan: no more than one large museum per day, mixed with an outdoor landmark walk and a break. Walking was the real effort constraint: the Mall is much longer on foot than it looks, so days were grouped by area to limit walking between sites.

General lesson: ask how long the group actually stays engaged and how far they comfortably walk, then plan by area, not by a list of attractions.

## Highlights and activity menu
Right after the Group Profile, the planner showed about 10 one-line DC highlights (big museums, monuments at night, a pro game, a day trip). The user ticked the ones that appealed, which told the planner what to research in depth.

The activity menu then presented about 15 options across Landmarks, Museums, History and Kids, each with a link, time, cost tier, booking note and a pre-filled “My pick.” Examples of booking notes it flagged: some landmarks require timed tickets or reservations that can sell out, and some tours need advance requests. The user answered in one line (“Accept, but 9 → Skip, 15 → Must”); only then was the draft itinerary built, with a DRAFT visual itinerary.

## Key planning lessons
- Ask about trip type and interests before ability questions; don't assume outdoors.
- “Not sure” answers become Open decisions resolved with options at the right step.
- Check interests against destination highlights early, then let the user pick from a pre-filled menu before drafting; the planner's job is options, research and recommendations.
- Keep questions small: at most 4 per reply, one thing each, with lettered or clickable answers.
- Group days by area to control walking and transit time.
- Free attractions with timed tickets still go on the booking checklist.
- End every reply with a clear “Your input needed” list.
